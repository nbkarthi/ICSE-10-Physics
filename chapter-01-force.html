The same push can move a body or turn it

A force on a rigid body that is free to move makes it travel in a straight line. This is translational motion. A force on a body that is fixed at a pivot makes it turn about that point. This is rotational motion.

Try it yourself

Name one everyday object that shows translational motion and one that shows rotational motion. (Examples: a pushed trolley moves in a straight line; a ceiling fan or a door on its hinges turns about a fixed line.)

A force turns a body better the farther it is from the pivot

The turning effect of a force is called its moment, also called torque. moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot. The SI unit is newton metre (N m). The cgs unit is dyne cm.

Try it yourself

Pick the door, set F = 10 N and r = 40 cm, then raise r to 100 cm. Now set r = 0. Why can no force open a door by pushing at the hinge? (Answer: the moment is F × 0 = 0, so there is no turning effect.) Why is a spanner made with a long handle? (A longer r gives the same moment with a smaller force.)

Two conditions for a body to be in equilibrium

A body is in equilibrium when the forces on it produce no change in its motion. Two things must hold: (1) the resultant force is zero (translational condition), and (2) the sum of anticlockwise moments equals the sum of clockwise moments about the pivot (rotational condition). Try it yourself

Set both pulls to 4 N and choose "Moving right". Does the block stop? (Answer: no. With zero resultant force it keeps moving at the same velocity, which is dynamic equilibrium.) Now set the right pull to 6 N. What changes? (The resultant is 2 N to the right, so it speeds up and is no longer in equilibrium.)

All the weight acts as if it were at one point

Gravity pulls on every particle of a body. These many parallel pulls can be replaced by one force, the weight, acting at a single point called the centre of gravity (C.G.). About this point the moments of the weights of all the particles add up to zero. Its position depends on the shape and on how the mass is spread out. The syllabus asks for this idea qualitatively only.

Why does a metre rule balance on a finger only at the 50 cm mark? (Answer: its C.G. is at the middle. Under any other point the weight, acting at the C.G., has a moment about the support and tips the rule.)

Constant speed, yet always accelerating

A body moving in a circle at constant speed keeps changing direction, so its velocity keeps changing. A force toward the centre must act on it all the time: the centripetal force. This is a standard example of motion where the speed is constant although a force and an acceleration are present.
